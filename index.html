          10 · Method

          How the model works

          The graph. Each circle is a company, group or part of the economy. Each arrow means “if the source is hurt, the target is hurt.” Its weight (0 to 1) is our estimate of how much of the target's revenue, funding or value is tied to the source, built from the cited figures (for example, OpenAI's share of Oracle's backlog).

          The shock. Your cut is applied to the four sources of new AI money: venture capital, Big Tech capex budgets, debt and private credit, and sovereign / SoftBank money.

          The spread. We use a DebtRank-style rule from the financial-contagion literature (Battiston et al., 2012; Bardoscia et al.). Each round, only the new stress of a node is passed on: Δstress(target) = Δstress(source) × weight × amplifier × (1 − cushion) × 0.92. The amplifier reflects leverage and rises 35% once a node passes its fragility point (refinancing spirals). The cushion reflects real revenue and cash, and grows with the “real AI demand” assumption. The model runs until changes fall below 0.1 point.

          Time. Markets show ~85% of their final stress within 30 days; funding within 6 months; capex, chips and data centers within a year; jobs and the wider economy over 1 to 3 years. At 3 years part of the stress heals, more if real demand holds. These speeds are assumptions, informed by 2000–2003 and 2022.

          Dollar figures. “Market value at risk” assumes a stress score of 100 corresponds to an 80% fall, roughly Cisco and the Nasdaq from 2000 to 2002. GDP effects use the St. Louis Fed's estimate of AI investment's contribution to growth and a standard 3-cent wealth effect per dollar of stock wealth lost.

          What it can't do. It doesn't model new money arriving from elsewhere, management decisions, regulation, China, or a fall in AI prices that sparks new demand. It can't tell you when, or whether, a cut happens.
